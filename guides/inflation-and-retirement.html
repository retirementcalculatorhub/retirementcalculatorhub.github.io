Inflation and Retirement

How inflation erodes savings and income, why it matters more in retirement, and how to protect your plan.

Inflation is the slow rise in prices, and it is the most underestimated risk in retirement planning. A 3 percent annual inflation rate, which sounds small, halves the buying power of money in about 24 years. That means a retirement income that looks generous at 65 can feel tight at 85. This guide explains the maths, why it matters more after you stop working, and how to protect your plan.

The maths of inflation

Inflation compounds, just like investment returns, but in the opposite direction. At 3 percent a year, 100,000 dollars today is worth about 74,000 in ten years, 55,000 in twenty years, and 41,000 in thirty years, in buying power. At 2 percent, the fall is slower but still large: 100,000 becomes about 55,000 in thirty years. These figures assume steady inflation, and real inflation varies year to year, sometimes sharply. The calculator shows your projected savings in today's money, which is the honest way to read it.

Why inflation matters more in retirement

While you are working, your income usually rises with inflation, through pay rises, so the erosion is partly offset. In retirement, your income is fixed by your savings and any pension, and if the pension does not rise with inflation, your buying power falls every year. Healthcare costs, which rise faster than general inflation, make the problem worse. That is why retirement planning must be done in real terms, after inflation, not in nominal dollars that look impressive but buy less.

Worked example

A 65-year-old retires with 1,000,000 dollars and withdraws 40,000 a year, 4 percent. At 3 percent inflation, the 40,000 buys what 40,000 buys today in year one, but by age 85 it buys what about 22,000 buys today, and by 95 what about 16,000 buys today. If the withdrawals rise with inflation, the pot must fund larger payments each year, so it runs out sooner. Either way, inflation takes a large share of the plan, and ignoring it makes the projection look far better than it is.

Protecting your plan

The main protection is to invest for growth even in retirement, rather than holding everything in cash, because cash loses buying power steadily. A diversified portfolio with stocks, which have historically outpaced inflation over long periods, helps, though it brings volatility. Inflation-linked pensions and annuities, where available, protect part of your income directly. Delaying retirement by a few years also helps, because it shortens the period inflation has to work and increases the pot. And planning for a rising withdrawal, rather than a fixed one, keeps the budget honest.

What to assume in planning

A common planning assumption is 3 percent inflation, which is close to the long-run average in many countries, though recent years have shown it can be higher. Test your plan at 4 percent as well, to see how much the result changes. If the plan works at 4 percent, it is robust. If it only works at 2 percent, it is fragile, and you should save more or retire later. Inflation is not a detail to add at the end; it is central to the whole calculation, and every projection that ignores it is misleading.

Inflation across a retirement

Follow a fixed income of 30,000 dollars a year through a 30-year retirement at 3 percent inflation. In year one, it buys what 30,000 buys today. In year ten, it buys what about 22,300 buys today. In year twenty, what about 16,600 buys today. In year thirty, what about 12,400 buys today. The income has lost nearly 60 percent of its buying power, even though the number never changed. That is why a retirement plan must assume rising withdrawals, and why the calculator shows the result in today's money. A plan that ignores inflation looks comfortable on paper and feels tight in practice, and the gap only grows with time. Test every projection at 3 and 4 percent inflation, and plan around the higher figure.

Tip: At 3 percent inflation, prices double in about 24 years — a 40,000 dollar yearly withdrawal at 65 buys what about 22,000 buys today by age 85.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does inflation affect retirement?

It erodes buying power every year. At 3 percent, money halves in value in about 24 years, so a fixed retirement income buys less and less.

What inflation rate should I assume?

About 3 percent is a common planning figure. Test your plan at 4 percent too, to check it is robust.

How do I protect against inflation?

Keep part of your money invested for growth even in retirement, consider inflation-linked income where available, and plan for rising withdrawals.
