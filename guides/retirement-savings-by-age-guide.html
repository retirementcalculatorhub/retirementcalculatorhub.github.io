Retirement Savings by Age

The savings benchmarks for each decade, what they mean, and how to use them without panicking.

Savings benchmarks by age, such as having one year's salary saved by 30 and three times by 40, are popular because they turn a vague goal into a number. They are useful as rough guides, but they are also easy to misread, because they assume a particular income path and retirement age. This guide explains the common benchmarks, where they come from, and how to use them sensibly.

The common benchmarks

A widely quoted set of benchmarks suggests saving one times your annual salary by 30, three times by 40, six times by 50, and eight to ten times by 60. Another version suggests 10 times salary by 67. These figures come from assuming you save about 15 percent of income from your 20s, earn a typical return, and retire at 67 with spending of about 70 to 80 percent of pre-retirement income. They are a reasonable picture for a steady career, but they are not a law of nature.

What the benchmarks assume

The benchmarks assume you start saving in your 20s, your income rises steadily, and you retire at 67. If you started late, took time out for children, changed careers, or plan to retire early, the benchmarks will be wrong for you, and that is not a failure. They also assume a particular spending level in retirement, which may be higher or lower than yours. Use them as a rough check, not as a pass or fail test.

Worked example

Take someone earning 50,000 dollars a year. The benchmarks say 50,000 saved by 30, 150,000 by 40, 300,000 by 50, and 400,000 to 500,000 by 60. If they have 100,000 at 40, they are behind the benchmark, but they are not doomed: saving 700 a month from 40 to 65, at 7 percent, adds about 567,000, while the existing 100,000 grows to about 572,000, bringing the total to about 1,139,000. The benchmark tells them they are behind; the calculator tells them what to do about it, and the second number is the useful one.

If you are behind

Being behind a benchmark is common, and it is fixable. The levers are saving more, retiring later, spending less in retirement, or a combination. Raising your saving rate by 5 percent of income, working two extra years, and trimming retirement spending by 10 percent together can close a large gap. Do not try to close it all at once with a heroic saving rate that collapses; a steady increase, reviewed yearly, is more likely to last. And remember that benchmarks ignore other retirement income, such as state and employer pensions, which reduce what you need saved.

If you are ahead

Being ahead is a good problem. It gives you options: retire earlier, save less, or spend more in retirement. Before cutting contributions, check the assumptions: is your spending estimate realistic, have you allowed for healthcare, and is your return assumption cautious? If the answer is yes, you can ease off, but keep saving something, because plans change and a buffer is valuable. The benchmarks are a guide, and the goal is a comfortable retirement, not a perfect score.

Checking yourself against the benchmarks

Take a 45-year-old earning 60,000 a year with 120,000 saved. The benchmark says three times salary by 40, or 180,000, and six times by 50, or 360,000, so they are behind. But the benchmark assumes a steady career from the 20s, and this person started saving at 35. What matters is the plan: saving 900 dollars a month from 45 to 65 at 7 percent adds about 469,000, while the existing 120,000 grows to about 485,000, bringing the total to about 953,000, well above ten times salary. The benchmark said behind, but the plan says well ahead, because the monthly figure is high and the horizon is long. Use the benchmark as a prompt to calculate, not as a verdict, and let the calculator, not the benchmark, decide whether the plan works.

Tip: Common benchmarks suggest one year's salary saved by 30, three times by 40, six times by 50, and eight to ten times by 60 — rough guides, not pass-or-fail tests.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I have saved by 40?

A common benchmark is three times your annual salary, but it assumes you started in your 20s and retire at 67. Use it as a rough check, not a verdict.

What if I am behind the benchmark?

It is fixable: save more, retire a little later, or plan to spend a little less. The calculator shows what each lever does.

Do benchmarks include pensions?

Usually not. State and employer pensions reduce the amount you need saved, so a benchmark can overstate your target.
