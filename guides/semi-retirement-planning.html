Semi-Retirement Planning

How to wind down gradually with part-time work, what it does to your savings, and how to plan the transition.

Semi-retirement means reducing work gradually rather than stopping all at once: working part-time, freelancing, or switching to a less demanding role, while drawing some income from savings. It is increasingly popular, because it eases the financial pressure and the lifestyle shock of full retirement at the same time. This guide explains how it works and how to plan it.

What semi-retirement looks like

Semi-retirement takes many forms. Some people reduce their hours with the same employer, working three days a week instead of five. Others leave a full-time job and take part-time or freelance work in the same field, or in a completely different one. Some run a small business. The common thread is a mix of earned income and savings income, which together cover spending, while the savings are drawn down more slowly than in full retirement. The mix can change over time, with work tapering off as savings grow or as energy falls.

The financial benefit

Part-time income makes a large difference to a retirement plan. Earning 15,000 dollars a year for five years after full retirement age is worth 75,000 dollars of income, and it also means five fewer years of full withdrawals from savings, which lets the pot keep growing. A person who would need 1,000,000 dollars to retire fully at 65 might need only 750,000 to semi-retire at 62 with part-time income, because the income covers part of spending and the savings last longer. Planning the transition

Talk to your employer early about reducing hours, because many employers are open to it and some have formal phased retirement schemes. Check how part-time work affects any pension: some pensions reduce if you draw them while still working, and some do not. Check tax too, because a mix of earned income and pension income can push you into a higher bracket. Plan the timetable: which year you reduce hours, by how much, and when you expect to stop entirely, and review it yearly as circumstances change.

The lifestyle benefit

Semi-retirement eases the shock of stopping work. You keep structure, colleagues and purpose, while gaining time for hobbies, travel and family. Many people find the gradual transition happier than a sudden stop, and it gives time to discover what retirement life is actually like before committing to it fully. It also keeps skills and contacts fresh, which makes returning to work easier if you need to, though most semi-retirees find they do not want to.

Risks to watch

The main risk is health: part-time work still requires health, and an illness can end it sooner than planned, so do not build a plan that only works if you can work until 75. Another risk is that part-time work pays less than expected, or is hard to find in your field, so test the market before committing. Keep an emergency fund, and keep the savings plan going during semi-retirement, because the savings still need to last for the years after work ends entirely.

A phased timetable, worked out

Take a 60-year-old earning 60,000 a year full-time who wants to semi-retire. Year one: reduce to four days a week, earning 48,000, and draw 5,000 from savings to cover the gap. Year two: reduce to three days, earning 36,000, and draw 12,000. Year three: reduce to two days, earning 24,000, and draw 20,000. Year four: stop entirely, and draw the full amount from savings and pensions. The gradual reduction lets the savings last longer, because withdrawals start small and grow, and it lets the person adjust to the new lifestyle in stages. The timetable is reviewed each year, and if health or markets change, the pace can be slowed or quickened. A phased plan is flexible in a way that a sudden stop is not.

Tip: Earning 15,000 dollars a year part-time for five years is worth 75,000 dollars of income — and it lets your savings keep growing instead of being drawn down.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is semi-retirement?

Reducing work gradually rather than stopping at once: part-time, freelance or a less demanding role, with income from both work and savings.

How does semi-retirement help my savings?

Part-time income covers part of spending and delays full withdrawals, so the pot lasts longer and needs to be smaller.

What should I check before semi-retiring?

How it affects your pension, your tax, and whether part-time work is available in your field. Talk to your employer early.
