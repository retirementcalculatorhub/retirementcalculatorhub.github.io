Employer Match Explained

How employer matching works, why it is the best return in retirement saving, and how to make sure you get the full match.

An employer match is money your employer adds to your retirement plan when you contribute, and it is the best return available in retirement saving, because it is an immediate and guaranteed gain. Many people miss part of it by contributing too little, which is like turning down a pay rise. This guide explains how matching works, with examples, and how to make sure you get all of it.

How matching works

A typical match works like this: your employer adds a percentage of your salary to your retirement plan, up to a limit, when you contribute. A common example is a 100 percent match on the first 3 percent of salary you contribute, plus 50 percent on the next 2 percent, so contributing 5 percent of salary gets you an extra 4 percent from your employer. The exact formula varies by employer and by country, and the details are in your plan documents, but the principle is the same: your contribution triggers an extra contribution from your employer.

Worked example

Take a salary of 50,000 dollars a year, with a match of 100 percent on the first 3 percent and 50 percent on the next 2 percent. Contributing 5 percent, or 2,500 a year, gets you 1,500 from your employer in the first 3 percent, plus 500 on the next 2 percent, a total of 2,000 a year extra. Contributing only 3 percent gets you 1,500, so you leave 500 a year on the table. Over 30 years, that 500 a year, at 7 percent, is worth about 47,000 dollars. The cost of contributing too little is large, and it is entirely avoidable.

Why it is the best return

A 100 percent match is an immediate doubling of your money, before any investment return. No investment can guarantee that, and it is risk-free in the sense that the match is paid as soon as you contribute. Even a 50 percent match is an immediate 50 percent return. That is why financial advisers say to contribute at least enough to get the full match before doing anything else with retirement saving, including paying extra into other accounts or investing elsewhere.

Vesting and conditions

Some employers require you to work for a period before the matched money is fully yours, a process called vesting. A common schedule vests the match gradually over three to five years. If you leave before you are fully vested, you may lose part of the match, though your own contributions are always yours. Check your plan's vesting schedule, and factor it into decisions about changing jobs. Also check whether the match is paid on every contribution or only up to a salary limit.

Getting the full match

Check your plan documents for the match formula, and set your contribution to at least the level that gets the full match. If you cannot afford that today, raise your contribution by 1 percent each year until you reach it, and direct pay rises into the plan before they become spending. Review the match whenever you change jobs, because the new employer's formula will be different. And remember that the match is part of your pay: leaving it unclaimed is the same as taking a pay cut.

Missing the match, added up

Take a salary of 60,000 dollars with a match of 100 percent on the first 3 percent and 50 percent on the next 2 percent. Contributing 5 percent gets the full match of 4 percent, or 2,400 dollars a year. Contributing only 2 percent gets 1,200, so 1,200 a year is left on the table. Over 30 years, that 1,200 a year, at 7 percent, is worth about 121,000 dollars. The cost of contributing too little is not the 1,200 a year, but the 121,000 it would have become. Check your plan's formula today, and set your contribution to at least the level that gets the full match. It is the simplest and most valuable change in retirement planning, and it takes five minutes.

Tip: A 100 percent employer match is an immediate doubling of your money — no investment can guarantee that, which is why getting the full match comes before any other retirement saving.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an employer match?

Money your employer adds to your retirement plan when you contribute, usually a percentage of your salary up to a limit.

How much should I contribute to get the match?

At least enough to get the full match. Check your plan documents for the formula, and raise your contribution gradually if you cannot afford it today.

What is vesting?

The schedule over which matched money becomes fully yours. Your own contributions are always yours, but the match may vest over three to five years.
