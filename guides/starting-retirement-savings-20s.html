Starting Retirement Savings in Your 20s

Why your 20s are the most valuable decade for retirement saving, and how to start with small amounts.

Your 20s are the most valuable decade for retirement saving, not because you can save the most, but because your money has the longest to compound. Small amounts saved now can be worth more at retirement than larger amounts saved later. The challenge is that your 20s are also the decade of low pay, student debt and big expenses. This guide explains how to start anyway, with amounts that fit a young budget.

Why time beats amount

A 25-year-old who saves 200 dollars a month until 65, at a 7 percent return, ends with about 525,000 dollars, having contributed 96,000. A 35-year-old who saves 200 dollars a month until 65 contributes 72,000, less money, and ends with about 360,000, far less in total. The younger saver wins despite saving less each month, because the money had ten more years to compound. That is the whole argument for starting in your 20s: time is the one input you cannot buy back later.

How much to save

A common guideline is to save 15 percent of your income for retirement, including any employer contribution, but that is a target to grow towards, not a starting point. In your 20s, even 5 percent is a good start, and the habit matters more than the amount. If your employer offers a matching contribution, contribute at least enough to get the full match, because that is an immediate doubling of your money. Increase the percentage by 1 percent each year, or with each pay rise, and you will reach 15 percent without ever feeling a cut.

Where to start

If your employer offers a retirement plan, start there, especially if it matches contributions. If not, open a retirement account yourself and set up an automatic monthly transfer for the day after payday, so the money leaves before you can spend it. Choose a low-cost diversified fund rather than trying to pick individual investments, because the evidence favours diversification and low fees over stock-picking. Keep an emergency fund separate, so a surprise bill does not force you to raid retirement savings.

Balancing debt and saving

Student debt and other loans compete with saving, and the balance depends on the interest rate. High-interest debt, such as credit cards, should be cleared first, because the interest costs more than investments are likely to earn. Low-interest student debt can be paid steadily while you save, because the return on saving is likely to be higher than the interest on the debt. Do both where you can: a small retirement contribution plus steady debt payments beats either one alone.

The habit that matters

The most valuable thing you can build in your 20s is the habit of saving automatically. Set the transfer, forget about it, and let time do the work. Check the balance once a year, increase the amount when your pay rises, and do not panic when markets fall, because a fall in your 20s is a buying opportunity, not a disaster. Your 20s are for starting, not for finishing, and a small, steady start now is worth more than a large, late one.

A 20-something's first year, planned

Take a 24-year-old earning 30,000 dollars a year. They contribute 5 percent, or 125 dollars a month, and the employer matches half, adding about 62 dollars, for a total of 187 dollars a month. After a year, they have contributed 1,500 and the employer has added about 750, and with a modest return the balance is about 2,300. The amount is small, but the habit is set, and the money has 40 years to compound. Each year, they raise the contribution by 1 percent of salary, so by 30 they are saving 10 percent, and by 35, 15 percent. The gradual rise means they never feel a cut, and the early start means the money has the longest possible time to grow. A small, automatic start beats a large, delayed one.

Tip: A 25-year-old saving 200 a month until 65 ends with about 525,000 dollars at 7 percent; a 35-year-old saving 300 a month ends with about 367,000. Time beats amount.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I save for retirement in my 20s?

Aim towards 15 percent of income including any employer match, but start with what you can, even 5 percent. The habit matters more than the amount.

Should I pay off debt or save for retirement?

Clear high-interest debt first. Low-interest debt can be paid steadily while you save, because investment returns are likely to be higher.

What if I can only save a little?

Save it anyway. Small amounts in your 20s can be worth more at retirement than larger amounts saved later, because of compounding.
