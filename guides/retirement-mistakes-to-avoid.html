Retirement Mistakes to Avoid

The most common errors in retirement planning, from starting too late to ignoring inflation, and how to fix each one.

Most retirement mistakes are not dramatic; they are quiet habits that add up over decades: starting late, saving too little, ignoring fees, or forgetting inflation. The good news is that most of them are fixable, especially if they are caught early. This guide covers the mistakes that cost the most, and the fix for each one.

Starting too late

The most expensive mistake is waiting to start. Every year of delay costs more than the last, because compounding needs time, and the missing years are the ones where growth would have been largest. The fix is to start now, with any amount, even 50 dollars a month, and to increase it with each pay rise. A small start today beats a large start in five years, and the habit of saving automatically matters more than the amount.

Saving too little

The second mistake is saving a token amount and never raising it. A 5 percent saving rate sounds responsible, but it is unlikely to fund a comfortable retirement on its own, and it often stays at 5 percent for decades while income rises. The fix is to raise the rate by 1 percent a year, or to direct each pay rise into saving before it becomes spending. After a few years, the rate reaches 15 percent or more without ever feeling like a cut.

Ignoring fees

A 1 percent annual fee sounds trivial, but over 30 years it can cut the final total by about a fifth, because the fee compounds just like the return. The fix is to choose low-cost funds, to check the fees on every account, and to avoid expensive products that promise more than they deliver. Fees are one of the few things in investing you can control completely, and controlling them is free money.

Forgetting inflation

A projection that ignores inflation looks impressive and is misleading: a million dollars in 30 years buys far less than a million today. The fix is to always read projections in today's money, which our calculator shows, and to plan withdrawals that rise with inflation. Test the plan at a higher inflation rate, such as 4 percent, to check that it still works.

Cashing out when changing jobs

When people change jobs, they are often tempted to cash out a small retirement pot, especially if it looks like a windfall. The cost is large: taxes, penalties, and the loss of decades of compounding on that money. A 10,000 dollar pot cashed out at 30 would have grown to about 115,000 by 65 at 7 percent. The fix is to leave the money invested, or to roll it into the new employer's plan or a personal retirement account, and to treat job changes as a transfer, not a payday.

The cost of cashing out, worked out

Take a 30-year-old who changes jobs and cashes out a retirement pot of 15,000 dollars, paying tax and a penalty that leaves about 10,000 in hand. The 15,000, left invested at 7 percent, would have grown to about 172,000 by 65. The 10,000 in hand is spent within a year, on a car or a holiday, and is gone. The true cost of the decision is not the tax or the penalty, but the 172,000 that never happened. The fix is simple: when you change jobs, roll the pot into the new employer's plan or a personal retirement account, and treat the transfer as paperwork, not as a payday. The money is for the future, and the future arrives faster than it feels like at 30.

Tip: A 1 percent annual fee can cut the final total by about a fifth over 30 years — fees are one of the few things in investing you can control completely.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the biggest retirement mistake?

Starting too late. Every year of delay costs more than the last, because compounding needs time.

How much do fees matter?

A lot. A 1 percent annual fee can cut the final total by about a fifth over 30 years.

Should I cash out my pension when I change jobs?

No. A 10,000 pot cashed out at 30 would have grown to about 115,000 by 65 at 7 percent. Roll it into the new plan instead.
