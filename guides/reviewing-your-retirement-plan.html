Reviewing Your Retirement Plan

How to review your retirement plan once a year, what to check, and when to change course.

A retirement plan is not a document you write once and forget. Income changes, markets move, plans change, and the plan must be reviewed regularly to stay honest. An annual review takes an hour, and it is the single most useful habit in retirement planning. This guide explains what to check, in what order, and when a review should lead to a change.

When to review

Review once a year, at the same time each year, such as January or your birthday, so it becomes a habit. Also review after any big change: a new job, a pay rise, a house move, a marriage or divorce, a child, or an inheritance. Do not review weekly or monthly, because short-term market moves are noise, and reacting to them usually makes things worse. An annual rhythm is frequent enough to catch problems and calm enough to ignore noise.

What to check first

Start with the basics: how much have you saved, how much are you contributing, and what is the balance compared with last year? Then update the calculator with your current age, savings, contribution and target, and read the new projection. Compare it with last year's projection: is the date moving closer or further away? If it is moving further away, find out why: lower contributions, poor returns, or a higher target, and decide what to change.

Check the assumptions

Review the return and inflation assumptions: are they still reasonable? A 7 percent return and 3 percent inflation are common planning figures, but they should be tested, not assumed. Check the fees on your accounts, and whether a lower-cost option is available. Check your investment mix: as you get closer to retirement, you will gradually shift towards more stable investments, but that shift should be gradual, not sudden. And check your target: has your spending changed, or have your plans for retirement age changed?

Check the other pieces

A retirement plan is more than a savings balance. Check your emergency fund: is it still three to six months of expenses? Check your insurance: life, health and disability cover, and whether they still fit. Check any debts, especially high-interest ones, and whether they are shrinking. Check your will and your beneficiaries, because these are easy to forget and costly to get wrong. And check your state and employer pension entitlements, because they are part of the plan too.

When to change course

Change the plan when the review shows a real gap, not when markets have a bad month. If the projection is behind, the levers are saving more, retiring later, or spending less in retirement. Choose the one that hurts least, and make the change gradually: a 1 percent rise in contributions, or a one-year delay in retirement, is easier than a dramatic overhaul. If the projection is ahead, you have options: retire earlier, save less, or keep going for a larger margin. Write down the decision and the reason, so next year's review can judge whether it worked.

An annual review, step by step

Do the review in five steps, in order. First, write down the current balance, the monthly contribution, and the target, and compare them with last year's figures. Second, run the calculator with the updated numbers and note the new projected total and the new monthly figure. Third, check the assumptions: is 7 percent return and 3 percent inflation still reasonable, and are the fees still low? Fourth, check the other pieces: emergency fund, insurance, debts, and beneficiaries. Fifth, decide on one change, if any, such as raising the contribution by 1 percent or delaying retirement by a year, and write it down with the reason. The whole review takes an hour, and the written record lets next year's review judge whether the decision worked.

Tip: Review once a year, at the same time each year. Do not review weekly — short-term market moves are noise, and reacting to them usually makes things worse.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I review my retirement plan?

Once a year, plus after any big life change such as a new job, a pay rise, a move, or a marriage. Do not review weekly.

What should I check in a review?

Savings balance, contributions, the updated projection, the return and inflation assumptions, fees, investment mix, and your target.

When should I change my plan?

When the review shows a real gap, not after a bad month in the markets. Change gradually: a 1 percent rise in contributions or a one-year delay.
