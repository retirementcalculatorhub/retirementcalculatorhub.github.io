How to Use a Retirement Calculator

A step-by-step guide to projecting your retirement savings from your age, savings, contributions and return.

A retirement calculator turns a few facts about your money into a projection: how much you are likely to have when you retire, and what it will be worth after inflation. The maths is straightforward, but the answer depends on the assumptions you choose, especially the return rate. This guide walks through each input, explains what it means, and shows how to read the result.

Start with your ages

Enter your current age and the age you plan to retire. The gap between them is the time your money has to grow, and it is the most powerful input in the whole calculation: a 35-year horizon gives compounding far more time to work than a 10-year one. Be realistic about the retirement age: if you plan to retire at 65 but might work part-time until 67, run the calculator for both, because two extra years of contributions and growth make a large difference. The calculator accepts retirement ages from 40 to 80.

Enter your current savings

Enter everything you have saved for retirement so far, in one number: pension pots, retirement accounts, and any savings you intend to use in retirement. Do not include an emergency fund or money set aside for other goals, because the projection assumes the whole amount stays invested until retirement. If your savings are spread across several accounts, add them up first. A round number is fine: the projection is an estimate, and a few hundred dollars either way changes the result by very little.

Choose your monthly contribution

Enter the amount you save each month for retirement, including any employer contribution if your plan has one. Be honest about what you can sustain: a figure you can keep for years beats a heroic figure you abandon after six months. If your contributions rise with your salary, enter today's figure and remember the projection is conservative, because it assumes the amount never rises. You can run the calculator again with a higher figure to see what a pay rise could do.

Pick a return and an inflation rate

The return rate is the most uncertain input. A common planning figure is 7 percent a year before inflation, which is roughly the long-run average return of a diversified stock portfolio in many markets, though real returns vary widely year to year. Inflation is often assumed at about 3 percent a year. The calculator shows the result both in future dollars and in today's money, so you can see what the projection is really worth. Try a lower return, such as 5 percent, to see how much the result depends on the assumption.

Read the result in two ways

The calculator gives a headline figure, the projected total at retirement, and a second figure, the value in today's money after inflation. The second figure is the honest one: a million dollars in 35 years buys far less than a million today. It also shows how much you contributed and how much came from growth, which usually surprises people: over a long horizon, growth often contributes more than the contributions themselves. If the result is lower than you hoped, the levers are saving more, retiring later, or both, and the calculator lets you test each one.

A worked example from start to finish

Take a 35-year-old with 20,000 dollars saved, contributing 500 dollars a month, planning to retire at 65, and assuming a 7 percent return and 3 percent inflation. The horizon is 30 years, or 360 months. The monthly rate is 7 percent divided by 12, about 0.583 percent. The current savings grow to about 162,000 dollars. The contributions, with growth, add about 610,000, for a total of about 772,000. Adjusted for 3 percent inflation over 30 years, that is about 318,000 in today's money. The example shows the two readings side by side: 772,000 sounds like a lot, and 318,000 is the honest figure. Try the same numbers at a 5 percent return, and the total falls to about 505,000, or about 208,000 in today's money, which shows how much the answer depends on the return assumption.

Tip: Try a lower return, such as 5 percent, to see how much the result depends on the assumption — the return rate is the most uncertain input in the whole calculation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does a retirement calculator work?

It projects your current savings forward with compound growth, adds your monthly contributions with growth, and adjusts the result for inflation.

What return should I assume?

A common planning figure is around 7 percent a year before inflation, but real returns vary. Try 5 percent too, to see the range.

Is the projection a guarantee?

No. It is a projection based on your assumptions. Real returns vary year to year, and the result is a planning guide, not a promise.
