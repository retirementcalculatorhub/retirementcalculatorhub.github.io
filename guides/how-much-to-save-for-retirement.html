How Much to Save for Retirement

How to work out your retirement savings target, from your spending to the 25-times rule, with worked examples.

The first question in retirement planning is how much you need, and the answer starts not with a round number but with your spending. A person who spends little needs far less saved than a person who spends a lot, even at the same income. This guide explains how to estimate your target from your own spending, with the rules of thumb that turn it into a number.

Start with your annual spending

Work out what you spend in a year, excluding any costs that will disappear in retirement, such as a mortgage that will be paid off, and adding costs that will appear, such as more travel or healthcare. A common starting point is 70 to 80 percent of your pre-retirement income, because you no longer pay work costs, payroll taxes on the same income, or retirement contributions. But your own spending is a better guide than any percentage: track it for a few months and annualise it.

The 25-times rule

A widely used rule says you need about 25 times your annual retirement spending saved, because that supports a withdrawal rate of about 4 percent a year, which has historically lasted 30 years in many scenarios. If you expect to spend 40,000 dollars a year in retirement, the target is about 1,000,000 dollars. If you expect to spend 30,000, the target is about 750,000. This is a rule of thumb, not a guarantee, and it assumes a diversified portfolio and a 30-year retirement. Use it as a starting target, not a final answer.

Worked example

Take a 40-year-old earning 60,000 dollars a year who spends 45,000 a year. In retirement, they expect to spend 35,000 a year, after the mortgage is paid off. Twenty-five times 35,000 is 875,000 dollars. They have 50,000 saved and can contribute 500 a month. Using the monthly contribution calculator with a 7 percent return, they would need to save about 730 a month to reach 875,000 by 65, or about 570 a month if they retire at 67. The example shows how the target, the timeline and the monthly figure connect.

Adjusting for your situation

The rule changes with your plans. Retiring early needs a larger multiple, because the money must last longer. A pension or other guaranteed income reduces the amount you need saved, because it covers part of your spending. High healthcare costs raise the target. Inflation matters too: a target of a million dollars in 30 years is worth far less than a million today, so think in today's money and let the calculator adjust. Revisit the target every few years, because spending, income and plans all change.

What if the target feels impossible

A large target can be discouraging, but it is a direction, not a deadline. Start with what you can save, even 100 dollars a month, and increase it when your income rises. Small amounts compound surprisingly well over decades: 200 dollars a month from age 30, at a 7 percent return, grows to roughly 360,000 dollars by 65. The most important step is starting, and the second is increasing contributions with each pay rise, before the extra money becomes part of your spending.

A target for a 30-year-old, worked out

Take a 30-year-old earning 55,000 dollars a year and spending 40,000. In retirement, they expect to spend 32,000 a year, after the mortgage is paid off and work costs end. Twenty-five times 32,000 is 800,000 dollars. They expect 15,000 a year from a state pension, leaving 17,000 to come from savings, so the target falls to 25 times 17,000, or 425,000. They have 10,000 saved and 35 years to go. Saving 300 dollars a month at 7 percent gives about 655,000 by 65, more than the target, so the plan works with room to spare. The example shows how the target shrinks once guaranteed income is counted, and why the spending figure, not the salary, is the starting point.

Tip: Start from spending, not income: 25 times your annual retirement spending is a common target, because it supports about a 4 percent yearly withdrawal.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much do I need to retire?

A common rule is 25 times your annual retirement spending. If you expect to spend 40,000 a year, the target is about 1,000,000.

Is 25 times spending a guarantee?

No. It is a rule of thumb that has historically supported a 4 percent withdrawal for about 30 years. It is a starting target, not a promise.

What if I cannot save enough?

Start with what you can, even 100 a month, and raise it with each pay rise. Small amounts compound well over decades.
