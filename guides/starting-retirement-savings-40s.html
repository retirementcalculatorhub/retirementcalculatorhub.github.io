Starting Retirement Savings in Your 40s

How to catch up on retirement saving in your 40s, when time is shorter but income is usually higher.

Starting retirement saving in your 40s feels late, and in one sense it is: you have missed the cheapest decade of compounding. But it is far from too late. Your 40s are often your highest-earning years, you may have fewer debts, and you still have 20 to 25 years before retirement. This guide explains how to catch up, how much to save, and what to prioritise.

The maths of starting at 40

A 40-year-old who saves 500 dollars a month until 65, at a 7 percent return, ends with about 405,000 dollars, having contributed 150,000. Saving 1,000 a month gives about 810,000. The numbers are lower than for a 25-year-old, but they are still substantial, and they show that the monthly amount matters more now than it did earlier. Use the monthly contribution calculator to find the figure that reaches your target, and treat it as a plan, not a judgement.

How much to save now

The 15 percent guideline still applies, but many people starting in their 40s need more, often 20 to 25 percent of income, to catch up. That sounds like a lot, but it becomes manageable if you raise it gradually: start at 15 percent, add 1 percent each year, and direct pay rises and bonuses into saving before they become spending. If your employer matches contributions, get the full match first, because it is the fastest way to close the gap.

Priorities in your 40s

First, clear high-interest debt, because it costs more than investments earn. Second, build an emergency fund of three to six months of expenses, so a surprise does not derail saving. Third, maximise retirement contributions, especially any employer match. Fourth, review your mortgage: paying it off before retirement reduces the income you need in retirement, which lowers your target. Education costs for children compete with all of this, and there is no single right balance, but retirement saving should not be paused entirely for them.

Investing in your 40s

With 20 or more years to retirement, you can still invest mainly in diversified stocks, which have historically returned more than bonds over long periods, though with more volatility. As retirement approaches, you will gradually shift towards more stable investments, but that shift belongs in your 50s and 60s, not your 40s. Keep fees low, diversify, and avoid trying to time the market. A single low-cost diversified fund is enough for most people.

Catching up without panic

It is easy to panic at 40 and either save recklessly or give up entirely. Neither helps. A steady plan, reviewed once a year, beats a dramatic one that collapses. Consider working a few years longer if you enjoy your work, because each extra year adds contributions, adds growth, and shortens the retirement the money must fund. And remember that retirement income also comes from other sources: state pensions, employer pensions, and part-time work all reduce the amount you need saved. The target is a guide, and a good plan is one you can keep.

A catch-up plan for a 42-year-old

Take a 42-year-old earning 70,000 a year with 20,000 saved, who wants 800,000 by 65. The horizon is 23 years. The current savings grow to about 100,000 at 7 percent, leaving 700,000 to come from contributions. The monthly amount needed is about 1,030 dollars, or 18 percent of income. That is a lot, but it becomes manageable in steps: start at 800 a month, add 100 a month each year, and direct the next two pay rises into saving. By 48, the contribution reaches 1,400 a month, and the plan is ahead of schedule. The example shows that a catch-up plan is a matter of steady increases, not a single heroic sacrifice, and that the employer's match, if any, makes the target easier still.

Tip: At 40, the monthly amount matters more than it did at 25: saving 1,000 a month until 65 gives about 810,000 dollars at 7 percent, versus about 405,000 for 500 a month.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 40 too late to start saving for retirement?

No. You still have 20 to 25 years, and your income is often at its highest. The monthly amount matters more now than starting early did.

How much should I save in my 40s?

Aim for 20 to 25 percent of income if you are catching up, building up gradually from 15 percent and directing pay rises into saving.

Should I work longer to catch up?

It helps a lot. Each extra year adds contributions and growth, and shortens the retirement your savings must fund.
